## Scope

Publication Gate v0.1に沿い、以下を独立確認した。

- short-term sleep efficacy

## Claim reconstruction

### C001 — behavioral sleep interventions after about 6 months can improve short-term sleep outcomes

PASS with heterogeneity.

Reuter et al. (2020) reviewed 12 original intervention studies and concluded that behavioral treatment is promising for infant behavioral sleep problems mainly above 6 months, while no individual intervention was replicated across more than one qualifying full-scale study.

Stanzel et al. (2025 volume; first published December 2024) included 20 studies and reported broadly mild-to-moderate improvements in unsettled infant behavior, including night waking / cry-fuss behavior, with substantial heterogeneity. Its search was updated only through May 2023, so publication year is not treated as search recency.

Chae et al. (2024) reviewed 11 actigraphy-based studies and found fairly consistent improvement in awakenings and sleep-onset latency; only two studies were low risk of bias.

Sources:
https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.15182
https://doi.org/10.1002/icd.2554
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37671613/

### C002 — sleep training substantially increases total sleep duration

PASS as not supported as a large average effect.

Fangupo et al. (2021) included 32 RCTs in children age 0–5 and found nocturnal sleep duration increased by mean 9 minutes/night (95% CI 4.1–13.8). 24-hour sleep change was +8.6 minutes (95% CI -2.7 to 19.8), not clearly different, and daytime sleep was unchanged.

This is a broad child-sleep intervention estimate, not a technique-specific infant “sleep training” effect. The Review appropriately uses it to reject a large-average-duration claim, not to estimate the effect of graduated extinction.

Uzdil et al. (2025), 4 digital-intervention RCTs, similarly found improvement in longest continuous sleep but not clear improvement in total night-time sleep; all included studies were high risk of bias and certainty was low.

Sources:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34029803/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41110255/

### C003 — graduated extinction / bedtime fading necessarily increase infant physiological stress

PASS as not supported by the available small RCT; evidence limited.

Gradisar et al. (2016), N=43 infants age 6–16 months, randomized graduated extinction, bedtime fading, and sleep education. Sleep latency improved in both behavioral groups; graduated extinction also improved awakenings / wake after sleep onset. Salivary cortisol showed small-to-moderate declines in the behavioral groups compared with control.

This trial does not support a claim of necessary cortisol increase. Its size is too small to establish broad safety across methods or detect rare harms.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27221288/

### C004 — studied behavioral sleep interventions cause long-term emotional / attachment harm

PASS as not supported by available follow-up evidence, not “proven universally safe.”

Gradisar et al. found no significant 12-month group differences in emotional/behavioral problems or secure/insecure attachment.

Price et al. (2012) followed a cluster-randomized intervention: original sleep-problem subgroup N=326, age-6 follow-up N=225 (69%). No clear intervention-control differences were found across measured child emotional/conduct outcomes, chronic stress, parent-child relationship, disinhibited attachment, or maternal mental health.

Attrition, sample size, intervention specificity and the limited number of long-term studies prevent a universal safety claim.

Sources:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27221288/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22966034/

### C005 — the same efficacy conclusion is established under 6 months

CHANGE REQUIRED: classify as mixed / insufficient for extension.

Douglas & Hill (2013) reviewed behavioral sleep interventions in the first six months and did not establish improvement in the maternal/infant outcomes considered; the authors also raised possible unintended consequences.

Crichton & Symon (2016) reviewed the same broad age boundary and identified 11 studies, 8 of which reported improved infant sleep after educational behavioral programs; they reached a more favorable conclusion.

Reuter et al. (2020) subsequently still listed treatment interventions in children below six months as an important knowledge gap.

These reviews differ in methods, intervention definitions and interpretation. The correct publication-level conclusion is not “no evidence” or “effective,” but that the evidence is mixed and does not justify mechanically extending the >6-month conclusion.

Sources:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24042081/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26836644/
https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.15182

### C006 — one universally superior sleep-training method exists

PASS as not established.

The major syntheses combine heterogeneous components and do not establish one universal winner. Reuter explicitly notes that multicomponent interventions prevent clean component attribution.

### C007 — safe-sleep guidance can be relaxed to facilitate sleep training

PASS as not supported.

Current Japanese Children and Families Agency guidance states that infants under 1 year should be placed supine for sleep. AAP 2022 safe-sleep policy recommends supine positioning, a firm flat noninclined surface, room sharing without bed sharing, and avoidance of soft bedding and overheating.

The independent update search found no newer AAP policy replacing the 2022 safe-sleep recommendations; 2024 AAP point-of-care material continues the same principles.

Sources:
https://www.cfa.go.jp/policies/boshihoken/kenkou/sids
https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2022-057990

### C008 — parental mood / burden may improve

PASS as partially supported / heterogeneous.

Recent meta-analyses report improvements in maternal mood, mental health or parenting confidence, but intervention content is heterogeneous and effects cannot be attributed to one sleep-training technique.
